CHOOSE YOUR BUILD
Topics
Five starting points below. These are harder than they look. Each one hides a real problem, an algorithm, a
correctness trap, or a genuine calculation that most apps get wrong. That hidden problem is the whole point.
Don't try to build all of it. Nail the core, go deep on the hard part, and be ready to explain it. You're not limited
to these. If you have a better idea, or want to put your own spin on one, go for it. We want to see what excites
you.

01
Errand Route Planner
Plan a set of errands and get the smartest order to run them in.
CORE FEATURES
Add stops, and an ordered route.
THE HARD PART
Ordering stops to minimise travel is a classic hard problem. Even a rough, sensible solution requires you to
think about the trade-offs and explain why your ordering beats just doing the errands in the order they were
added.
02
Renewal Reminder
One place for road tax, licence, passport, insurance, and subscription renewals that nudges you before anything
lapses.
CORE FEATURES
Add items with their renewal dates, and a reminder view of what's coming up.
THE HARD PART
Everything renews on a different cycle, and a reminder is only useful if it lands with enough lead time to act.
Deciding when to nudge, and not drowning the user in alerts, is the actual design problem.
03
Cash-Flow Forecaster
Plug in your income and recurring bills, and see your projected balance across the month so you know when
things get tight.
CORE FEATURES
Add income and recurring expenses, and a forecast view.
THE HARD PART
Bills land on different cycles (weekly, monthly, that one annual renewal), and the useful output is the exact day
your balance dips lowest. Modelling those overlapping cycles correctly is where it lives.
04
Cheapest Basket
Enter your shopping list and find out which shop, or which split between two shops, gets you the whole basket
for the least.
CORE FEATURES
Keep prices for your regular shops, and an optimised basket result.
THE HARD PART
One shop is cheaper on some items and pricier on others, so the best answer isn't obvious. And a second stop

only pays off if the saving beats the extra trip. Deciding that trade-off is the challenge, not the list.
05
Savings Goal Allocator
Set a few savings goals with target amounts and dates, and see how to split your spare cash each month to hit
them.
CORE FEATURES
Add goals with targets and deadlines, and a monthly allocation plan.
THE HARD PART
Your surplus is limited and the goals compete. The useful app works out a split that lands each goal on time,
and tells you plainly which goals aren't reachable rather than pretending they are.

Tech stack is your call. Web, mobile, desktop, or CLI. Use whatever you're comfortable with.
READ THIS CAREFULLY
How to approach this
This matters more than the topic you pick.
Plan for roughly 8 to 12 hours of work. The build window exists so you can fit this around classes and life, not so
you can pour 60 hours into something huge. We are not scoring effort. A small, complete, well-understood app
beats a large, impressive one you can't explain.
Go deep, not wide. Two solid features you fully understand are worth more than ten half-built ones. Pick one
meaningful problem and do it properly.
Understand what you build. By the end, you should be able to explain what your app does, why you made the
choices you made, how it works under the hood, and where it falls short. If you can't explain a part of it, that
part isn't finished.
Here's the difference we're looking for, made concrete:
Candidate A
Spent 60 hours. The app looks polished and packed with features. But they can't explain how it works or why
they made key decisions.
Candidate B

Spent 12 hours. The app is small but the core workflow is complete. They followed a clear process, can
explain every choice, and checked that it actually works.
We hire Candidate B.
THE BAR
What we're looking for
We care about how you think, not just what you build. Specifically:
• A systematic approach. Evidence that you planned, worked in steps, and made deliberate decisions rather
than piling things on until it ran.
• Understanding the problem. You grasp what the app is for, who uses it, and where its limits are.
• Understanding your own solution. You can explain how it works, even in broad strokes, and why it's built
the way it is.
• Sound reasoning. You can talk through the choices you made, the alternatives you weighed, and the trade-
offs.
• Honest reflection. You know what's unfinished, what's uncertain, and what you'd check before real users
touched it.
• Ownership. You stand behind everything you submit, including the parts an AI helped you write.
What we're not looking for
Skip these. They won't help your score, and chasing them usually hurts it:
• Decorative polish. A beautiful UI on top of a workflow you don't understand.
• Feature volume. More features is not more impressive. It's usually less.
• Cinematic demos. A flashy video won't cover for a shaky product. Keep the demo simple and honest.
• Time-maxxing. Spending the whole window on this to look committed. We'd rather you spent 10 focused
hours.
BE HONEST ABOUT IT
Using AI
Use AI. We expect it, and how you use it is a real part of how we evaluate you. Don't hide it, and don't be shy
about it in your pitch. We would much rather hear “I used AI here, and here's how I checked it” than have you
pretend you didn't.
What we want to see is thoughtful use, matched to the task:
• Delegating the right work to AI, and keeping the parts you should own
• Reading and understanding what it gives you before you ship it
• Verifying that its output actually works

• Catching, rejecting, or reworking suggestions when they're wrong
• Avoiding complexity you don't need
AI use runs on a spectrum, from simple autocomplete, to asking a chatbot questions, to letting an agent run and
reviewing the result, to more structured setups. You don't need the fanciest workflow. Someone using simple
tools carefully will beat someone running an elaborate agent they can't explain. Reach for the approach that fits
the job, and be ready to talk about why.
Show your working
Commit as you go. A commit history that shows how your work progressed helps us understand your process,
and makes your pitch easier. This isn't decisive and it's fine if you're new to Git, but small, meaningful commits
beat one giant “final” dump at the end.

GOOD TO KNOW
FAQ
Do we build something new, or can we submit an old project?
If you go with one of our topics, build it fresh from scratch. Or you're welcome to submit a passion project
you've already built.

Who owns the work?
You do, fully. Keep it as a portfolio piece, keep improving it, or turn it into something bigger.
How much time should this take?
Plan for roughly 8 to 12 hours. The build window is for scheduling flexibility, not for maximizing hours. We don't
reward time spent.
Do I have to use the suggested topics?
No. Use them, remix them, or propose your own. All submissions are judged the same way.
Is there a required tech stack?
No. TypeScript is recommended, but use what you're comfortable with. Just make sure you can finish in time.
Two features, or more?
Two solid, well-built features are enough. Go deeper, not broader. Add more only if it genuinely strengthens the
app and you can still finish.
Can it be a CLI instead of a GUI?
Yes. Web, mobile, desktop, or CLI all work. Just justify the choice: the use case, the target user, and why that
interface fits.
Am I allowed to use AI?
Yes, and we expect it. How you use it is part of the evaluation, so use it thoughtfully and be ready to explain
what you delegated, what you kept, and how you checked the output.
Should I make my commit history look impressive?
No need to game it. Just commit naturally as you work. Meaningful, incremental commits help us follow your
process, but they don't decide the outcome.
What carries the most weight in scoring?
How you think. We review the code, the product, and your presentation, but the deciding factor is whether you
understand what you built and can reason about it.
Does design and UX matter, or just functionality?
Both. We build products, so it has to work and the experience has to make sense. But polish never substitutes
for understanding.
